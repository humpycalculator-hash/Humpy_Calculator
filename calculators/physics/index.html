About the Physics calculators

These calculators cover motion, force, energy, electricity, waves and gravity using standard school formulas. Each one shows the formula, the substitution and the final answer with its unit.

Use SI units (metres, kilograms, seconds) unless a calculator gives you a unit menu. Mixing units, such as grams with kilograms, is the most common reason for a wrong answer.

Worked examples

Force

Mass: 10 kg

Acceleration: 3 m/s²

F = m × a = 10 × 3

Answer: 30 N

Kinetic energy

Mass: 2 kg

Speed: 3 m/s

KE = ½ × m × v² = ½ × 2 × 9

Answer: 9 J

Ohm’s law

Current: 2 A, resistance: 6 Ω

Leave voltage blank

V = I × R = 2 × 6

Answer: 12 V

Tips for accurate results

  • In Ohm’s Law, leave exactly one box blank and fill the other two. The calculator finds the missing value.
  • The Speed calculator lets you choose distance and time units and converts them for you. For example, 150 km in 2 hours is about 20.83 m/s (75 km/h).
  • Angles for Work Done and Projectile Motion are entered in degrees, not radians.

Frequently asked questions

Which units do the physics calculators use?

Mostly SI units: metres, kilograms, seconds, newtons, joules, volts, amperes and ohms. Where a calculator offers a unit menu, such as distance and time for speed, choose the unit you have and it converts for you.

What value of gravity is used?

Standard gravity, 9.80665 m/s², is used by Free Fall and as the default in Potential Energy. In Potential Energy you can type a different value of g, for example for another planet.

Does the speed calculator give average speed?

Yes. It uses v = d ÷ t, so the answer is the average speed over the whole distance and time you enter, shown in m/s.

Why does Ohm’s Law ask me to leave one box blank?

Ohm’s Law links three values: voltage, current and resistance. You need to know two to find the third, so the calculator solves for whichever box you leave empty.

How are the results rounded?

Answers are shown to up to four decimal places. Very large or very small numbers are shown in scientific notation, such as 1.5e12.

Results are for learning and quick checks. For exams, lab reports or engineering work, follow your instructor’s rules for units and significant figures.
